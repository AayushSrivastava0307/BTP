The clock count is set by the image, not the kernel. A 5×5 kernel fires 25 multiplies per clock where a 2×2 would fire 4 — but both stream the image exactly once. That is what unrolling the kernel into hardware buys: the kernel size decides how many PEs you spend, not how long you run.

1 · Input 6 × 6

Grey = not yet read. Tinted = still remembered by the line buffer. Solid = entering this clock.

2 · Line buffer — the memory that makes it work

Each pixel enters the first delay line and shuffles along one box per clock. Six boxes = one image row, so what falls out the end is the pixel directly above. The lines are chained: the second one is fed by the first’s output, giving the pixel two rows up.

Total storage: 2 shift registers × 6 = 12 pixels. tap 2 costs nothing — it is a direct wire off the incoming pixel, not stored. (conv1 is the same shape scaled: 4 × 32 = 128 pixels.)

3 · Kernel

Loaded once at the start, then held still for the whole image.

4 · The 9 PEs — weight × pixel

Each box: the weight it holds × the pixel arriving, and which output that product is heading for. The three PEs in a row are usually feeding three different outputs — that is the pipeline.

Only PE0 is wired to the line buffer. Read the “from” line in each box: PE1 takes its pixel from PE0, PE2 from PE1 — each hop costing the 2 clocks of the delay registers inside a PE. So the buffer needs just 3 output wires, not 9 (5, not 25, for conv1). Six of these nine PEs never touch it.

Who is each PE working for?

The answer to “how can output (0,0) use clocks 26–28 and output (0,1) use clocks 27–29?” — they never share a PE. Read the diagonals: each output walks PE0 → PE1 → PE2 on three consecutive clocks, and the moment PE0 passes its partial sum along it is free to start the next one. Reading down a column instead gives you one output’s three vertical rows, computed at the same instant.

ClockPE0 · kernel col 2PE1 · kernel col 1PE2 · kernel col 0Finishes

Each row of this table is one clock tick, and every non-blank cell is 3 real multipliers firing at once (one per kernel row). So a clock with two filled cells has 6 multipliers live, feeding two different answers. Nothing is time-shared — that is why an answer can leave every clock even though each one takes 3 clocks to assemble.

The first pixel arrives at clock 12 — why is there no answer until clock 30?

A 3×3 answer needs three rows of the picture, and rows arrive one after another. Pixel 12 (start of row 2) doesn't turn up until clock 24. Then the pixels still have to walk along the chain of PEs, which takes a few more clocks. After that first answer, though, one comes out every single clock — the wait is paid once, not per answer.

Why are the weights loaded backwards?

Pixels arrive newest-first as they march in, so the PE nearest the entrance meets the rightmost pixel of the window. To pair correctly it must therefore hold the rightmost weight. Reversing the load order is what makes weight and pixel line up; it is a fix, not a quirk.

Are all 9 PEs working on the same window at once?

No — and this is the bit people trip on. Look at the "heading for" line in each PE: at any instant the three PEs in a row are usually part-way through three different outputs. Each answer is assembled over three clocks as it slides along, while new ones start behind it. Like a car factory: several cars on the line at once, one rolling off per minute.

The last pixel goes in at clock 49 — why does it keep running to 57?

That tail is the flush, and it exists because the machine is a pipeline. When the last pixel arrives, the last four answers are still inside the array: their pixels have to finish walking along the chain and their partial sums have to catch up. Stop the clock at 49 and you throw away outputs (3,0) to (3,3), which actually emerge on clocks 51–54. With nothing left to feed, the design pushes in zeros to shove the real data out — harmless, because any window containing them lies outside the image and gets masked. The RTL budgets 2K+2 for this: 8 clocks here, 12 for conv1. Add 2 more for the DONE state and its registered ready, and you get 57.

Why are some clocks thrown away?

When the sliding window reaches the right-hand edge, the next positions would wrap onto the following row and mix pixels that aren't neighbours. The hardware still computes them — it's a pipeline, it can't stop — so a valid flag simply discards them. Here that's 2 wasted slots per row.

Each pixel is read only once — how, when it belongs to up to 9 windows?

That's exactly what the line buffer buys. Instead of going back to memory each time a pixel is needed again, the design keeps the last two rows in a small on-chip queue. The pixel is fetched once and re-used from the queue for every window it takes part in.

How do only 9 PEs produce 16 outputs?

The 9 PEs are the kernel, not the picture. The same 9 weights apply at every position — that's what makes it a convolution — so you build the kernel in hardware once and slide the picture past it. Positions are handled by time, not by more hardware.

Nine multiplies per answer, but only three clocks of array time — and a new answer still emerges every clock, because the next window is already right behind. Same ledger shape at both scales: 11 + 36 + 8 + 2 = 57 here, 27 + 1024 + 12 + 2 = 1065 for conv1.
